# Enterprise Architecture: Foundations and Modelling

### Introduction to Enterprise Architecture
**Enterprise Architecture (EA)** is a crucial discipline and practice that fundamentally bridges the gap between an organisation's strategic aspirations and its practical implementation.
It achieves this by systematically adapting and evolving the **enterprise infrastructure**, which encompasses the entire business organisation, its application landscape, and its underlying data and technological infrastructure.
Effectively, EA serves as a managerial approach focused on ensuring continuous **value creation**, fostering **enterprise coherence**, and achieving robust **strategic alignment**.

The primary purpose of Enterprise Architecture is to analyse and provide clear direction for changes within this enterprise infrastructure.
This ensures that all resources within the organisation contribute to its overall purpose.
**Value creation** refers to the effective utilisation of an enterprise's resources to fulfil its stated purpose.
**Enterprise coherence** signifies that diverse resources are integrated and aligned, forming a cohesive system where they collaborate rather than operate in isolation.
This concept has a narrower interpretation as **Business/IT Alignment**, focusing on the integration of information technology (IT) and business resources to achieve organisational goals.
**Strategic alignment** ensures that the strategic decisions regarding resource allocation for value creation are translated into a blueprint for the organisation.
This blueprint enables effective value creation consistent with the overarching strategic direction and vision, directing resources towards common goals.
The enterprise infrastructure itself is a complex ecosystem comprising various resources including values, principles, policies, organisational structures, roles, processes, projects, data, information, knowledge, applications, services, systems, technology, materials, and human capital.

Within this context, the **enterprise architecture** (lowercase 'a') refers to an **artefact**, essentially a blueprint for the organisation that translates strategic resource choices into a tangible plan for value creation.
Any partial or complete representation of this blueprint is known as an **enterprise architecture description** or **enterprise architecture artefact**, serving as the principal tool for enterprise architects.
**Enterprise Architecture (EA)**, capitalised, represents the broader field of study and the comprehensive body of knowledge and tools dedicated to understanding, analysing, and designing an organisation's enterprise architecture.
As defined by Lankhorst et al. (2009), EA is "a coherent whole of principles, methods and models that are used in the design of an enterprise’s organisational structure, business processes, information systems and infrastructure."
This definition builds upon the general concept of architecture, which, according to ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2011, describes the fundamental organisation of a system embodied in its components, their interrelationships, their relationship to the environment, and the guiding principles for its design and evolution.
The term 'enterprise' in this context can refer to a single organisation (such as a company or government body), a specific part of an organisation (like a business unit), or even a collaboration of multiple organisations (e.g., a value network or supply chain).

**Enterprise Architecture Management (EAM)** is a distinct business and management field of study that focuses on the practical application of EA knowledge and tools.
It employs socio-behavioural approaches to ensure value creation, enterprise coherence, and strategic alignment in practice.
Conversely, Enterprise Architecture as an information systems discipline employs design-based research to develop innovative solutions for the practical application of enterprise architecture.
The **enterprise architecture capability** or **function** within an organisation refers to the established organisational structures, required skills, specific roles, defined responsibilities, comprehensive toolsets, and operational processes necessary to establish and maintain an architecture function effectively.
These capabilities can manifest in various organisational forms, from an independent internal company to a dedicated unit within an IT department, with diverse reporting lines and levels of independence.

### Modelling as a Core EA Skill
Central to EA practice are **EA artefacts**, which are vital for decision-making and communication among stakeholders.
These artefacts act as the tangible outputs of architectural work, enabling a shared understanding of the enterprise's structure and dynamics.
Creating these essential artefacts necessitates the use of robust conceptual modelling languages.
For business engineers, three conceptual modelling languages are particularly critical: **Unified Modeling Language (UML)** for software systems, **Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN)** for business processes, and **ArchiMate** for enterprise architecture.
ArchiMate stands out as the industry-wide international standard specifically designed for EA modelling.
Knowledge of UML is particularly beneficial for learning and precisely understanding ArchiMate, as ArchiMate's meta-model incorporates and reuses concepts from the UML meta-model.
Examples of such models include UML class diagrams, BPMN process diagrams, and various ArchiMate visualisations like value stream maps, capability maps, and layered enterprise architecture models.

#### The Basics of ArchiMate
**ArchiMate** is a comprehensive modelling language that supports an integrated approach to enterprise architecture modelling.
Unlike disparate architecture descriptions each using different notations for specific domains, ArchiMate allows for the modelling of relationships between elements spanning various architecture domains.
An **architecture domain** can be conceptualised as a distinct aspect of an enterprise requiring architectural decisions, such as business processes, products, organisational structures, information, applications, or digital infrastructure.
ArchiMate is a generic modelling language, meaning it is not tailored to specific industries or sectors, yet it includes built-in customisation mechanisms for controlled language extension.
It is a graphical modelling language, supporting the creation of model views visualised as diagrams and maps, providing clear visual representations of the enterprise architecture.
The ArchiMate 3.2 language, currently in use due to its tool support, defines 73 core concepts, comprising 60 elements, 11 relationships, and 2 relationship connectors.
Its meta-model notably reuses UML concepts such as composition, aggregation, association, and specialisation, maintaining similar semantics.

#### ArchiMate Language Structure
The **top-level hierarchy** of ArchiMate concepts begins with a **model**, which is composed of instances of **concepts**.
Concepts can be categorised into **elements**, **relationships**, and **relationship connectors**.
An **element** represents a fundamental building block defined in the ArchiMate meta-model.
A **relationship** defines a connection between a source and a target concept, typically elements.
A **relationship connector** links two or more relationships of the same type.
Elements themselves are further classified into **behaviour elements**, **structure elements**, **motivation elements**, and **composite elements**.

**Behaviour elements** represent actions, things that are performed or occur within the enterprise, while **events** denote state changes that may result from these actions.
**Structure elements** represent entities that exist within the enterprise.
These are subdivided into **active structure elements**, which are the actors performing behaviour, and **passive structure elements**, which are the objects upon which behaviour is performed.
ArchiMate also distinguishes between **external elements** and **internal elements**.
External elements provide a 'black-box' view of a system, representing the services it offers to its environment (external behaviour elements) and the interfaces through which these services are accessed (external active structure elements).
Internal elements, conversely, reveal the internal mechanisms, showing how services are created (internal behaviour elements) and by whom or what (internal active structure elements).

The main semantic relationships between behaviour and structure elements are crucial for understanding system dynamics:
Internal active structure elements are **assigned** to internal behaviour elements, indicating who or what performs which action.
External behaviour elements, often called **services**, are made accessible through **interfaces** (external active structure elements) that are assigned to them.
Internal active structure elements can be **composed** of interfaces and may be **served by** interfaces from other internal active structure elements.
Internal behaviour elements **realise** services and may in turn be **served by** services realised by other internal behaviour elements.
Behaviour elements **access** passive structure elements, illustrating the objects upon which the behaviour is performed.
Events, representing state changes, can either **trigger** behaviour or be **triggered by** behaviour.
Internal behaviour elements can be specialised into a **process**, which emphasises the ordered steps of an activity; a **function**, which highlights the competencies required for an activity; or an **interaction**, which describes collective behaviour performed by a collaboration of two or more internal active structure elements.

ArchiMate relationships are categorised into three types:
**Structural relationships** define the static construction or composition of elements, whether of the same or different types.
**Dependency relationships** illustrate how certain elements support or impact others.
**Dynamic relationships** describe temporal, causal, informational, or material dependencies between behaviour elements.
Relationship connectors, such as the **(AND) junction** and **(OR) junction**, allow for the combining of relationships, bearing conceptual similarities to BPMN's parallel and inclusive/exclusive gateways.
Similarly, the **triggering relationship** in ArchiMate is analogous to BPMN's sequence flow, indicating a temporal or causal progression.

To gain practical proficiency, students are encouraged to download and study the ArchiMate 3.2 specification, focusing on key chapters and sections, and to install the Archi software tool on their laptops for hands-on application.
